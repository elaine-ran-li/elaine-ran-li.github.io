Working Papers

  1. Ran Li, Yang Liu, Zhiya Zuo, and Weiquan Wang. "Emergent Fashionable Digital Technologies and Legitimacy for Digital Ventures in Nascent Markets: Evidence from the Web3 Industry." Major Revision at MIS Quarterly (UTD24, Latest manuscript available upon request)
    • Under 2nd Round Review
    • Selected for presentation at the PolyU Jointed University Summer Workshop on Information Systems 2023 (JUSWIS 2023)
    • Selected for presentation at the HKU Jointed University Summer Workshop on Information Systems 2024 (JUSWIS 2024)
    Abstract

    Digital ventures are major drivers in today's economy, yet they face high failure rates in nascent markets where they often struggle to gain legitimacy in the eyes of investors. To address this, many digital ventures embrace emergent fashionable digital technologies (EFDTs) to create a yet-to-come future to attract investors. EFDTs are digital technologies that are under development but collectively believed to have the potential to come into fashion. While existing literature has attested to the legitimacy benefits of adopting already fashionable technologies, the legitimation effects of engaging with EFDTs remain understudied. Drawing on the lens of prospective legitimation, we theorize the legitimacy effects of two forms of EFDT engagement: symbolic engagement and substantive engagement. We employed a multi-method design, comprising a panel data analysis of 276 Web3 ventures (2019 – 2022) and two online experimental studies. Our findings show that substantive engagement with EFDTs is a robust driver of legitimacy, whereas symbolic engagement increases legitimacy only when the venture is young or operates in highly nascent markets. Moreover, our experiments revealed a substitutive effect between the two forms of engagement when they target the same underlying technological capabilities of an EFDT. Our study advances the technology fashion literature by examining digital technologies in the emergent phase of the fashion lifecycle. We reveal nuanced relationships and differences in the effects of two forms of EFDT engagement on the legitimacy of digital ventures in nascent markets. We offer practical guidance for digital ventures seeking legitimacy in nascent markets.

  2. Yang Liu, Ran Li, and Weiquan Wang. "“Open Source” in Crowdsourcing Contests: A Knowledge Reuse Perspective." Target Journal: Management Science
    • Manuscript available upon request
    • Selected for presentation at the PolyU Jointed University Summer Workshop on Information Systems 2023 (JUSWIS 2023)
    • Selected for presentation at the HKU Jointed University Summer Workshop on Information Systems 2024 (JUSWIS 2024)
    Abstract

    The open-source movement has expanded beyond software to various fields, shifting its focus from merely providing free access to software source code to promoting community-driven collaboration and innovation. This shift has popularized crowdsourcing contests as a means to leverage open source for collaborative, open-ended problem-solving, encouraging contestants to build upon each other’s solutions while competing against each other. Nevertheless, the impact of open source on contestant performance remains controversial in the extant crowdsourcing contest literature. One plausible reason is that the extant literature tends to focus on whether contestants are exposed to open-sourced solutions, largely ignoring how contestants reuse others’ open-sourced solutions through which vicarious learning occurs. We address this controversy by drawing on the vicarious learning theory and knowledge reuse (KR) perspective. By analyzing 178 contests on Kaggle, we reveal several nuances in the effects of different ways of KR on contestant performance in open source-encouraged crowdsourcing contests. First, repeatedly reusing the open-sourced solutions from the same contributor (i.e., exploitative KR) is detrimental to contestant performance, whereas widely reusing open-sourced solutions from various contributors (i.e., exploratory KR) is beneficial. Second, contestants’ direct experience in the current contests (i.e., one’s own trial-and-error experience in solving the open-ended problem) helps alleviate the negative effect of exploitative KR, but it reduces the positive effect of exploratory KR. Third, the contest deadline-induced time pressure on contestants can strengthen the positive effects of exploratory KR but does not influence the effects of exploitative KR. A survey of Kaggle participants also validated the theoretical mechanism underlying the effect of the two forms of KR. This study advances our theoretical understanding of the pros and cons of open source in crowdsourcing contests. It offers practical implications for effectively applying open source practices on the crowdsourcing contest platforms.
